## Definition and Classification
- Radiation intestinal injury divides into acute and chronic, typically with a 3–6 month boundary.
  - Acute: mucosal inflammation.
  - Chronic: intestinal wall ischemia and fibrosis.
- By location: small intestine (enteritis) and rectum (proctitis).
- Four subtypes: acute radiation enteritis, acute radiation proctitis, chronic radiation enteritis, chronic radiation proctitis.
- Focus: chronic radiation small intestinal injury, treated primarily with resectional surgery.

## Epidemiology
- Incidence varies: 5–55% of all radiotherapy patients; 80% of pelvic radiotherapy patients have long-term bowel habit changes, 20% have moderate–severe symptoms affecting quality of life, only ~55% seek medical attention.
- Actual incidence likely higher due to underreporting and misdiagnosis (as other inflammatory, infectious, or drug-induced bowel diseases), often diagnosed only after complications (obstruction, fistula, perforation, bleeding) arise.

## Pathogenesis and Pathology
- Early changes (within hours): epithelial apoptosis, lamina propria inflammation, crypt abscess.
- Late changes: vasculitis, small vessel ischemia, submucosal fibrosis, bowel wall thickening.
- Two typical pathways: (1) mucosal ulcer → perforation → fistula → abdominal abscess; (2) wall fibrosis → stricture → bowel obstruction.
- 70% of surgical patients present with small bowel obstruction.

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis
- Main symptoms: bowel obstruction, fistula, diarrhea, gastrointestinal bleeding, anemia, malnutrition.
- Diagnosis: based on radiotherapy history and gastrointestinal symptoms. Key differential diagnoses: primary tumor recurrence/metastasis, postoperative adhesions.
- **Diagnostic examinations**:
  - CT: assess severity of abdominal fibrosis and locate obstruction.
  - Contrast studies (GI/fistula): identify strictures and evaluate fistulas.
  - Colonoscopy/contrast enema: evaluate concomitant radiation proctitis.
  - For iron-deficiency anemia, endoscopy (small bowel enteroscopy, colonoscopy, capsule endoscopy) to exclude ulcer bleeding.
- Distinction from metastatic tumor may sometimes only be confirmed by laparotomy.

## Prevention and Acute Phase Management
- Severity of acute radiation injury predicts chronic injury. Minimizing acute mucosal damage is important.
- **Prevention strategies**: Reduce small bowel radiation dose and volume. Physical methods: prone positioning during radiotherapy, bladder filling, belly board, surgical techniques (e.g., absorbable mesh sling). Pharmacologic prevention (5-aminosalicylic acid, sucralfate, glutamine, probiotics, antioxidants, keratinocyte growth factor) lacks high-quality evidence.
- **Acute phase management**: Self-limiting mucosal inflammation. Treatment: dietary modification, anti-diarrheal agents (opioid receptor blockers, somatostatin), antibiotics for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, glucocorticoids, parenteral nutrition if necessary.

## Chronic Radiation Intestinal Injury Treatment

### Surgical Treatment
- **Indications**: RTOG late radiation morbidity grades 3 and 4 (obstruction, bleeding, bowel necrosis, perforation, fistula) (https://www.rtog.org/ResearchAssociates/AdverseEventReporting/RTOGEORTCLateRadiationMorbidityScoringSchema.aspx).
- Approximately 1/3 of chronic radiation intestinal injury patients require surgery.
- **General principles**: Chronic ischemia and fibrosis are irreversible; definitive resection and reconstruction is optimal. Palliative surgery (bypass, exclusion) reserved for selected patients.
- **Surgical approach**:
  - Resection including the ileocecal region may lower reoperation rate by using healthy ascending colon for anastomosis outside the radiation field (Lefevre et al.), but increases long-term short bowel syndrome risk.
  - Acute phase: surgery only in emergencies; otherwise, optimize nutrition and wait for chronic phase to define disease extent.
  - **Damage control surgery** for emergencies, severe malnutrition, abdominal infection, extensive radiation injury, or dense adhesions: perform stoma or bypass without extensive resection; definitive surgery after recovery.
  - Main operative challenge: "frozen pelvis" – dense adhesions with loss of normal planes, high risk of injury to bladder, ureter, iliac vessels, sigmoid/rectum. Side-to-side anastomosis preferred for better blood supply.

#### Obstruction
- Adhesiolysis: separate only adhesions between affected bowel loops and adjacent organs (abdominal wall, bladder, sigmoid, uterus, retroperitoneum), not inter-loop adhesions.
- Laparoscopic approach: similar to rectal surgery port placement; adhesiolysis then intra- or extracorporeal anastomosis. Reduces wound complications. Conversion rate ~23%. Adhesion grading (see table below): grades 3–4 are relative contraindications for laparoscopy. Preoperative imaging may help reduce conversion.

**Table 1: Adhesion Grading for Laparoscopic Surgery in Radiation Enteritis**

| Grade | Description (affected bowel loops adherent to surrounding tissues/organs) |
|-------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 0     | No adhesions between affected loops and surrounding tissues/organs |
| 1     | Adhesions and fibrosis limited to right pelvic retroperitoneum and side wall; may involve sigmoid colon/rectum, no adhesion to bladder or anterior pelvic wall |
| 2     | Adhesions involve entire pelvis, including sigmoid colon/rectum; may involve bladder inferiorly, but no anterior pelvic wall adhesion; adhesions often severe and difficult to separate |
| 3     | Adhesions involve entire pelvis, including bladder and anterior pelvic wall; severe adhesions |
| 4     | Adhesions involve entire pelvis and extend upward to involve anterior abdominal wall; severe adhesions |

*Note: Does not include adhesions between affected small bowel loops.*

- After abdominoperineal resection or low anterior resection, close pelvic floor to prevent small bowel entrapment. In reoperation, omental flap can be used for pelvic floor closure.

#### Fistula
- Surgery complicated by infection and fluid corrosion; ileal fistula most common, then rectovaginal, enterovaginal, rectovesical, enterovesical.
- Definitive resection and reconstruction carries high failure rate; bypass, exclusion, and staged surgeries often preferred.

#### Gastrointestinal Bleeding
- Rare; usually a single deep small bowel ulcer. Preoperative localization: capsule endoscopy, angiography, colonoscopy; intraoperative endoscopy guides resection. Balance resection extent with bowel length preservation.

### Nutritional Treatment
- High malnutrition risk throughout treatment; causes include tumor, SIBO, short bowel syndrome, depression-induced poor intake. ~1/3 of surgical patients are malnourished. Up to 10.6% of long-term home parenteral nutrition patients have radiation injury.
- **Chronic malnutrition**: Screen and assess; start with enteral nutrition. For recurrent incomplete obstruction or fistula without surgery, use home oral nutritional supplements (isotonic, low-residue formula). Home parenteral nutrition is an option but not routinely necessary in China.
- **Perioperative nutrition**:
  - Preoperative: if enteral nutrition is not tolerated and surgery is indicated, proceed without delay; give parenteral nutrition to improve status.
  - Postoperative: gut function recovers slowly; continue parenteral nutrition, transition to enteral as tolerated. If residual small bowel ≤200 cm (short bowel syndrome criteria), create feeding access (gastrostomy) or distal limb intubation for fluid recycling in jejunostomy patients.
  - Discharge on oral supplements until weight recovery or second surgery. Long-term management for short bowel syndrome follows relevant guidelines.

## Surgical Complications Prevention and Management
- **Anastomotic leak**: Rate up to 30%; 40–60% require reoperation. Risk factors: hypertension, low platelets, massive intraoperative blood loss, high ASA grade, residual diseased bowel.
  - Key prevention: complete resection of diseased bowel, ensure healthy tissue for anastomosis. Optimal perioperative nutrition and timing of surgery.
- **Short bowel syndrome (SBS)**: Caused by extensive resection, accounts for 9.7% of SBS etiologies. 1-year survival 93%, 5-year 67%; most achieve intestinal adaptation and wean off parenteral nutrition.
  - Prevention: Ileocecal preservation technique – minimize terminal ileum resection, anastomose preserved terminal ileum to proximal bowel; consider protective proximal loop stoma to reduce both short-term and long-term complications.

## Follow-up
- Chronic radiation injury can manifest late (up to 30 years post-radiotherapy). Lifelong monitoring for recurrence and nutritional status.
- Rapid nutritional deterioration may signal recurrence; evaluate fully. If no tumor recurrence, reoperation after nutritional support may be needed.
