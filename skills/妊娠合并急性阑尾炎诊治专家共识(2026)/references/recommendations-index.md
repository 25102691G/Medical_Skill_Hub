## Clinical Issues and Recommendations

### 1. Acute appendicitis in pregnancy vs. non-pregnant: clinical presentation differences

**Recommendation 1** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): In early pregnancy, presentation is similar to non-pregnant. In mid‑late pregnancy, pain is located above McBurney’s point; guarding and right lower quadrant tenderness occur less frequently than in non-pregnant patients. Pay attention to these sign changes when evaluating acute abdomen in mid‑late pregnancy.

### 2. Laboratory tests for acute appendicitis in pregnancy

**Recommendation 2** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): Laboratory evaluation primarily includes blood routine and related inflammatory markers. Because white blood cell count rises physiologically with gestational age, its specificity for acute appendicitis is reduced; clinical assessment must rely on dynamic changes of multiple laboratory markers (e.g., neutrophil‑to‑lymphocyte ratio, C‑reactive protein, procalcitonin).

### 3. Imaging for acute appendicitis in pregnancy

**Recommendation 3‑1** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): Ultrasound is recommended as the first‑line imaging modality; however, its diagnostic value is limited in late pregnancy because the enlarged uterus often displaces the appendix into sonographic blind spots.

**Recommendation 3‑2** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): When ultrasound results are inconclusive or further evaluation is needed, MRI may be chosen.

**Recommendation 3‑3** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): In emergency situations where a prompt diagnosis is required, low‑dose CT may be cautiously used as an adjunct after carefully weighing safety and necessity.

### 4. Surgical vs. conservative treatment for acute appendicitis in pregnancy

**Recommendation 4** (Strong, Evidence Quality: High): For patients with a confirmed or highly suspected diagnosis and no surgical contraindications, proceed to surgery as soon as possible while providing active anti‑infective therapy. Conservative management is associated with higher risks of sepsis, septic shock, peritonitis, miscarriage, and preterm birth; treatment delay significantly increases maternal‑fetal complications.

### 5. Main pathogens and principles of antibiotic use

**Recommendation 5** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): The predominant pathogens are gram‑negative bacilli (e.g., *Escherichia coli*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*) and anaerobes (e.g., *Bacteroides* spp.). Start empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics covering these organisms immediately; adjust to a targeted, narrow‑spectrum regimen after susceptibility results are available. In critically ill patients with organ dysfunction or sepsis/septic shock, escalate to a higher‑level broad‑spectrum regimen early.

### 6. Choice of surgical approach

**Recommendation 6** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): Either open appendectomy or laparoscopic appendectomy may be chosen, with the decision based on gestational age, maternal‑fetal condition, complexity of disease, available resources, and surgeon expertise. Laparoscopy may reduce preterm birth, intrauterine fetal death, postoperative pain, hospital stay, and thrombotic/infectious risks, but in mid‑late pregnancy it may increase the risk of preterm labor; open surgery offers better exposure and hemostasis in certain situations.

### 7. Timing and mode of delivery

**Recommendation 7** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): Acute appendicitis itself is not an indication for cesarean delivery; the mode of delivery should be determined primarily by obstetric indications. For patients who are already in labor or at ≥34 weeks of gestation with complicated appendicitis (e.g., perforation, gangrene, abscess formation) or difficult appendiceal exposure, concurrent cesarean section with appendectomy may be considered after multidisciplinary team discussion.

### 8. Venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis

**Recommendation 8** (Strong, Evidence Quality: Moderate): Pre‑ and postoperative VTE risk assessment should be performed promptly. Lifestyle modifications and physical prophylactic measures (e.g., intermittent pneumatic compression) are recommended; pharmacological prophylaxis with low‑molecular‑weight heparin should be initiated when indicated (e.g., Caprini score ≥4, or ≥28 weeks with score ≥3). Reassess VTE risk once the transient surgical risk factor is removed.
